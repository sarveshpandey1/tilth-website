Marketing attribution for small businesses, explained simply

"Attribution" sounds like a word built to confuse founders into hiring someone. It's actually a simple idea: which marketing touchpoint gets the credit when a customer interacted with several before buying? Here's what that means in plain English, why your platforms will never agree, and the one thing attribution can't tell you however good it gets.

Anuja Founder, Tilth · 10+ years in performance marketing

Ask ten founders what "marketing attribution" means and most will give a vague answer involving dashboards and jargon. The actual idea is simple: a customer rarely buys from the very first ad they see. They might see an Instagram post, search your brand name a week later, click a Google ad, and finally buy after an email reminder. Marketing attribution is just the method for deciding which of those touchpoints gets credit for the sale — and that choice changes which channels look like they're working.

What "attribution" actually means, in plain English

Attribution answers one question: when a sale happens after several marketing touches, how much credit does each one get? It's not a measurement of reality so much as a rule you apply to messy reality, because there's no perfect way to know exactly how much each touchpoint mattered to the customer's decision. Different rules — different attribution models — will hand credit to different channels for the exact same sequence of events.

It matters because budget decisions get made on which channels "look like" they're working. If the model quietly overcredits one and undercredits another, you cut a channel that was contributing and double down on one that got lucky with the last click.

The models, and which ones still exist

Last-click

All the credit goes to whatever the customer clicked right before buying. Simple, but it systematically favours channels that close a sale — branded search, retargeting — over the blog post or influencer mention that started things.

Data-driven

Rather than a fixed rule, it uses patterns in your own conversion data to estimate how much each touchpoint contributed. The most realistic of the common models, provided the data feeding it is accurate.

First-click, linear, time-decay, position-based

Worth knowing mainly so you don't go hunting for them: Google removed all four from GA4 reporting in November 2023. Plenty of guides still describe them as GA4 options. They aren't.

So GA4 offers three models — data-driven, paid and organic last click, and Google paid channels last click — chosen at report level, with lookback defaulting to 30 days for acquisition key events and 90 days for everything else. Meta works differently again: standard, incremental and custom models at ad set level, where standard counts clicks within 1 or 7 days, views within 1 day of an impression, and engagements within 1 day.

Attribution doesn't tell you the truth. It tells you a story, consistently, according to a rule. The question is whether that rule is honest about how your customers actually buy.

A simple example: one customer, three touchpoints

A customer sees an Instagram ad on Monday, searches your brand name and visits on Wednesday without buying, then clicks a Google remarketing ad on Friday and purchases. Under last-click, Google remarketing takes all the credit. Under data-driven, it's split across all three based on how similar journeys tend to convert — probably giving Instagram real credit for starting things, even though it never got the final click.

Three things people call "attribution" that aren't the same

Most confused measurement conversations are these three used interchangeably.

  • The attribution model — a rule for splitting credit among touchpoints a system can see. An accounting convention, not a discovery.
  • The source of truth — the one system you agree to decide from. Usually the one closest to money, your orders or CRM, with platforms as directional inputs rather than rivals.
  • Incrementality — whether the sale would have happened anyway. No attribution model answers this, because all of them start from conversions that already happened.

Attribution can't prove incrementality

A channel receiving credit has not been shown to have caused anything. Branded search is the classic case: someone who already decided to buy searches your name, clicks the ad, converts, and the ad takes credit for a sale that was arriving anyway. Retargeting has the same problem in a different coat.

The platforms know this, which is why incrementality is now a separate product rather than a model setting — Meta's incremental attribution, in its own words, "uses machine learning models that predict whether a conversion is caused by an ad." The small-business version needs no machine learning: turn a channel off for a defined period and watch total orders, not that channel's reported conversions. Blunt, and it answers a question attribution structurally cannot.

Why your platforms will never agree

Google Ads, Meta and GA4 will report different numbers for the same week, and your accounts will differ from all three. That's expected. They use different models and windows; each counts conversions it was involved in, so the same sale gets claimed twice; Meta counts view-through and engage-through conversions GA4 wouldn't attribute the same way; and some data is modelled rather than observed — Google states plainly that it "uses modeling to estimate online key events that can't be observed directly," including for unconsented users under consent mode, and notes GA4 attribution data can keep updating for up to twelve days.

Consent is the gap underneath all of it: where visitors decline tracking the sale still happens but the touchpoints aren't recorded, and the system either models the gap or drops it — Google notes that without enough traffic to model confidently, key events go unreported or land in "Direct". Pick a source of truth, expect the others to disagree, and be able to explain the gap rather than close it.

The prerequisite nobody mentions: tracking has to work first

Attribution can only distribute credit among touchpoints it sees. If your Meta pixel is missing events or GA4 undercounts purchases, no model corrects for data that was never captured — it just produces a more convincing wrong answer. Confirm what's measured before debating how credit is split: that's GA4 conversion tracking and a basic ad spend audit, and it's why measurement is architecture rather than a dashboard.
