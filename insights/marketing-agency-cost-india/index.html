How much does a digital marketing agency cost in India?

It's the first question every founder asks and the hardest to get a straight answer to. Here's an honest breakdown of what a digital marketing agency costs in India — the ranges you'll actually be quoted, why no authoritative source for them exists, what separates fee from ad spend, and the questions that make two quotes comparable.

Anuja Founder, Tilth · 10+ years in performance marketing

Ask five agencies what they charge and you'll get five wildly different numbers — ₹15,000 here, ₹2,00,000 there — for what sounds like the same thing. The confusion is real and it's expensive: brands either overpay for a thin retainer or underpay for a package that quietly does very little. This guide sets out what a digital marketing agency costs in India, where those numbers come from, and how to make two quotes actually comparable.

First, an honest note about the numbers

There is no authoritative source for Indian agency pricing. No industry body publishes a rate survey. Every figure you'll find — including the range below — comes from agencies publishing their own guides, each with a commercial interest in where the number sits. Checking current published sources for this update, they disagreed substantially: retainer floors quoted anywhere from ₹5,000 to ₹25,000, ceilings from ₹2,50,000 to several lakh. So treat any range, ours included, as what you're likely to be quoted rather than what things cost. The structural part of this guide — what drives the price, and what to ask — is far more durable than the figures.

The range you'll actually be quoted

With that caveat in place: across current published Indian sources, monthly retainers cluster roughly between ₹25,000 and ₹2,50,000, broadly as follows.

Type of engagementCommonly quoted monthly fee
Freelancer / solo specialist₹15,000 – ₹50,000
Small business / early-stage startup₹25,000 – ₹75,000
Growing brand, multi-channel₹75,000 – ₹1,50,000

Agency fees only, drawn from current published market sources — market observation, not established fact, and not a Tilth price list. Outliers in both directions are common.

These are agency fees — the cost of strategy and execution. They almost never include the money you spend on ads, which is where most budgets actually blow up.

Agency fee vs ad spend — the part everyone confuses

Digital marketing pricing in India is really two numbers stacked together:

  • The agency fee (or retainer): what you pay the agency to plan, build and run the work.
  • The ad spend (media budget): what you pay directly to Google, Meta and other platforms to show your ads.

When an agency quotes "₹40,000 a month," ask the next question immediately: does that include ad spend? Almost always it doesn't — and the media budget is yours to lose if the measurement underneath it is wrong. Ask the same about tools, creative production and landing pages.

The fee is what you pay to run the engine. The ad spend is the fuel. Confusing the two is how budgets quietly double.

The four pricing models

  • Monthly retainer. A fixed fee for an agreed scope — most common, easiest to budget for. The risk is scope drift, so the scope document matters more than the number.
  • Project-based. A one-off fee for a defined deliverable: a website, a campaign build, an audit. Good for bounded needs, and for testing a relationship before committing to a retainer.
  • Percentage of media spend. Published bands in India commonly fall somewhere between roughly 10% and 20%, usually lower as spend rises and almost always with a minimum fee underneath — though sources disagree enough that you should treat the percentage as negotiable, not standard. The structural caution: it pays more when you spend more, which is a weak incentive at exactly the moment you want someone arguing for restraint.
  • Performance-linked or hybrid. A lower base plus a share of results. Attractive in principle, and only as honest as the tracking that measures the result — so the measurement needs to be independent of whoever gets paid on it. Agree the data source before the percentage.

What actually moves the price

Two quotes can differ by ten times for entirely legitimate reasons. Roughly in order of impact:

  • Number of channels. One channel done properly costs far less than an integrated programme — and is often the better early buy.
  • Creative volume. The most underestimated line. A few static assets a month and a continuous video testing cadence are different businesses.
  • Tracking and analytics work. Real engineering effort. Missing from the quote usually means missing from the work.
  • Landing pages and CRO. Whether anyone is allowed to change the page the traffic lands on, and whether building and testing it is in scope.
  • Geography. One city, pan-India, or India plus an international market in parallel.
  • Reporting complexity. A monthly summary costs less than dashboards, cohort views and a board pack.
  • Seniority and strategy time. Whether the people who pitched do the work — more often the gap between two identical-looking retainers than anything else.
  • Media spend under management. Bigger budgets mean more governance: often a lower percentage, a higher absolute fee.
  • Lifecycle and CRM scope. Email, WhatsApp and retention are a separate discipline; in or out changes the number materially.

Most of these are scope questions, not quality questions — which is the useful insight. You can't tell whether a price is fair until you know which of the nine it covers. The full span of what might be in scope is laid out across our growth marketing services, and that's a fair checklist to take into any agency conversation, including ones that aren't with us.

How to make two quotes comparable

Most founders compare the monthly figure and nothing else, which is how a cheap retainer wins and then disappoints. Five questions do most of the work:

  1. What's inside the fee and what's billed separately? Media, tools, creative, landing pages, tracking.
  2. Who does the work, and how much of their month do I get? Named people and rough hours, not titles.
  3. What happens in month one? A credible answer audits and fixes measurement before scaling spend. "We'll launch campaigns in week one" is a warning, not a feature.
  4. What are we measuring, and in whose system? If the agency's dashboard is the only scoreboard, you don't have one.
  5. What's the exit? Notice period, and who owns the accounts, pixels, tags and creative files afterwards. In writing.

We've written separately on the questions worth asking before you hire and on choosing between an agency, a freelancer and an in-house hire — often the real decision hiding behind a pricing question.

Why the cheapest option is often the most expensive

A very cheap retainer isn't automatically bad; it's automatically narrow. Something was left out to reach that price, and in our experience it's rarely the visible work — the posts, the campaign launches — and almost always the invisible work: tracking, attribution, targeting discipline. You save on the fee and lose a multiple of it on media spent against numbers nobody verified. When founders tell me their marketing "isn't working", the retainer size is rarely the cause; it's that the numbers underneath were never right. Which is why performance marketing that can't show you its own measurement isn't performance marketing.

How to budget if you're just starting

Rather than anchoring on a published range, work from two things you control. What can the business sustain for several months without flinching — because a relationship cancelled in month two costs the full fee and produces no conclusion. And can the fee and the media budget both run long enough to evaluate whatever you actually agreed to measure? Where that's bidding performance, Google's 30-conversion guidance is one reference point; if you sell few, large deals, whether those conversions became qualified pipeline will tell you more than the count. Either way, if the plan can't fund a fair evaluation of its own objective, the retainer size is the wrong thing to be negotiating. Then, before committing to anyone, get an independent read on whether your tracking and targeting are sound — which is exactly what a foundation audit is for.
