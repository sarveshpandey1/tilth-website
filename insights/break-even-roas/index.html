Break-even ROAS: how to calculate the ROAS your ads actually need

Every founder running paid media eventually asks whether a 3x is good. It isn't a question ROAS can answer on its own. Here's how to work out the ROAS at which your ads stop losing money, why gross margin gives you the wrong number, and the several things break-even ROAS still doesn't tell you.

Anuja Founder, Tilth · 10+ years in performance marketing

"Is a 3x ROAS good?" is one of the most common questions founders ask, and it has no answer. Not because it's a bad question, but because ROAS on its own is a ratio with half the information missing. The same 3x is excellent for a software business and quietly fatal for a brand selling discounted electronics. Break-even ROAS is the number that supplies the missing half: the point at which your ads stop losing money. This is how to calculate it, and — just as important — what it still won't tell you.

The short answer

Break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ your contribution margin. At a 50% contribution margin it's 2.0x. At 25%, it's 4.0x. At 70%, about 1.43x. That's the whole formula, and the arithmetic is the easy part — almost every mistake people make here is in the margin, not the division.

Five things people call "ROAS"

Most confused conversations about ad profitability are these being used interchangeably.

TermWhat it isWhat it doesn't tell you
Revenue ROASRevenue ÷ ad spend. The raw ratio.Whether any of that revenue was profit
Break-even ROASThe ratio at which contribution exactly covers ad spendAnything about fixed costs or company profit
Target ROASThe ratio you decide to run at, normally above break-evenWhether the target was set from evidence or hope
Platform ROASThe ad platform's attributed revenue ÷ its spendWhat actually settled in your bank account
Business profitabilityWhat's left after every cost, fixed and variableWhich channel caused it

Break-even ROAS is a media-level test. The last row is a company-level outcome. They are not the same question.

What break-even ROAS actually means

At break-even, one rupee of ad spend produces exactly enough contribution to pay for itself. Not profit — payback. You are running to stand still, and you would have the same bank balance if you hadn't advertised at all, minus the time.

That framing matters because it sets the floor correctly in your head. Break-even is not a target. It's the line below which you are certainly losing money, which is a different and much less ambitious claim.

The formula, and the margin that drives it

Contribution margin = contribution profit ÷ revenue
Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ contribution margin

Contribution profit is what's left from a sale after every variable cost except marketing. That last clause is the one people get wrong. Variable costs usually include cost of goods, shipping and fulfilment, payment gateway charges, marketplace or platform commissions, the discount you actually gave rather than the one on the price list, expected refunds and cancellations, and any sales commission or variable servicing cost. If a cost rises when you sell one more unit, it belongs here.

A note on language: "contribution margin" is used here as an operating framework for deciding ad spend, not as a formal accounting statement. Your accountant may define some of these lines differently, and this isn't tax or accounting advice. What matters for the decision is that you've subtracted everything that genuinely varies with volume.

A worked example

A D2C brand selling a ₹2,000 product:

LineAmount
Selling price₹2,000
Product cost (COGS)−₹700
Shipping and fulfilment−₹150
Payment gateway−₹50
Expected refunds and discount leakage−₹100
Contribution before marketing₹1,000
Contribution margin50%
Break-even ROAS (1 ÷ 0.50)2.0x

Illustrative arithmetic. Substitute your own lines — the output moves a long way on small changes.

Read it through once at 2.0x: ₹1 of ad spend produces ₹2 of revenue. At a 50% contribution margin, that ₹2 leaves ₹1 before marketing. That ₹1 exactly covers the ₹1 you spent. Nothing remains. The campaign is not failing and it is not succeeding — it is precisely neutral, and every fixed cost in the business is still unpaid.

Why gross margin gives you the wrong number

Say the same brand used gross margin instead and subtracted only COGS: ₹2,000 − ₹700 = ₹1,300, a 65% margin, implying a break-even ROAS of about 1.54x. A campaign running at 1.8x would look comfortably profitable. In reality it's below the true 2.0x floor and losing money on every order. The error is quiet, consistent, and scales exactly as fast as you do. Where shipping, gateway fees, commissions, discounts and returns are material — which is most of D2C — gross margin isn't a conservative approximation. It's the wrong input.

Break-even is a floor, not a target

Your target ROAS should normally sit above break-even, because break-even covers acquisition and nothing else. The gap between the two has to pay for whatever your business needs it to pay for, and that list is specific to you:

  • Measurement uncertainty — if your reported ROAS carries error, the floor needs a margin of safety above it.
  • Costs outside media — agency fees, creative production and tools, where those aren't already inside the ad spend figure.
  • Fixed costs — payroll, rent, software, and everything else that doesn't move with volume.
  • Actual profit — the business presumably exists to make some.
  • Cash position — a company with short runway needs a wider gap than one that can wait.

Which is why there's no universal buffer. "Always add 20%" is a made-up number that happens to be right for somebody. Work out what the gap has to absorb in your business, in rupees per month, and derive the target from that. Setting it deliberately rather than by habit is a large part of what performance marketing planning involves.

Beating break-even doesn't mean the business makes money

Worth stating flatly, because it's the most common misreading of this whole concept. Clearing break-even ROAS means your acquisition spend is covered at the contribution level you defined. It says nothing about payroll, rent, software subscriptions, agency fees excluded from media, creative production, debt service or tax. A business can run every campaign above break-even and still lose money for the year.

So hold two numbers, not one: media break-even, which this formula gives you, and company-level profitability, which it does not. Confusing them is how a brand ends up scaling confidently into insolvency.

Blended ROAS hides weak new-customer economics

This one costs D2C brands real money. A campaign can report 4x because existing customers — people who were going to buy anyway — are redeeming offers and converting through paid. The blended number looks healthy while the acquisition engine underneath it is losing money on every genuinely new customer.

If the point of the spend is acquisition, judge it on acquisition: new-customer revenue, new-customer CAC and first-order contribution, with repeat contribution tracked separately rather than folded in. Most ad platforms and ecommerce backends can split new from returning; if yours can't, that's the first thing to fix. The habit matters most for D2C and ecommerce brands, where repeat purchase is common enough to flatter almost any report.

When acquiring below break-even is defensible

Sometimes a first order should lose money. Subscription businesses, genuine consumables, contracted recurring revenue and validated cross-sell all produce customers worth more than their first purchase, and refusing to pay for them is its own mistake.

The test is evidence. It's legitimate when you have a measured repeat rate or retention curve from your own cohorts, a payback period you can state in months, and enough cash to survive that period — ideally with the whole thing stress-tested against a worse retention number than your best cohort produced. It is not legitimate when "we'll make it back on LTV" is doing the work. An LTV assumption with no cohort data behind it isn't a plan; it's the same loss, scheduled. Why your platform ROAS won't match finance

Two separate gaps, and it helps to name them apart.

The first is what the conversion value actually contains. A platform reports the value your tracking or integration sent it. Whether that figure is gross, post-discount or net of something else depends entirely on how the implementation was built — so don't assume it's always gross booked revenue, and don't assume it isn't. Check what yours sends before reasoning from it.

The bigger issue is what happens afterwards. A cancellation or return days later doesn't automatically revise the conversion that was already recorded; the correction has to be supplied. Google, for instance, documents two adjustment types for exactly this — restate, which "allows you to change the conversion value, but not the count of conversions", and retract, which removes a conversion and sets its value to zero, for cases it names as "canceled reservations or returned purchases". Both are uploaded by the advertiser, and online conversions can only be adjusted if you passed a transaction ID in the first place. Other platforms handle this differently again. So unless somebody built that correction loop, the dashboard keeps counting revenue your accounts never saw — and your real contribution is lower than it looks. There's no universal return rate to assume here; use your own.

The second is attribution. Platform ROAS is that platform's own attributed output, counted inside its own window and model. It is a vendor report from an interested party, not accounting truth, and it will disagree with GA4, your backend and your CRM — each of which counts differently again. That disagreement is normal and explainable rather than a fault. Reconcile platform-reported orders against your backend regularly, decide from the system closest to the money, and treat building that reconciliation as measurement work rather than reporting.

A practical checklist

  1. List every variable cost per order, including the discount you actually give and your real refund rate.
  2. Calculate contribution margin from those, not from gross margin.
  3. Divide 1 by it. That's your floor.
  4. Decide what the gap above the floor must absorb, in rupees per month, and set a target from that.
  5. Split new-customer from repeat performance before judging any campaign.
  6. Reconcile platform revenue against backend net revenue, and know the size of the gap.
  7. If you're deliberately running below break-even, write down the payback period and the cohort evidence for it.

Break-even ROAS — FAQs

The return on ad spend at which the contribution your sales generate exactly equals what you paid to generate them. Above it, acquisition spend is covered; below it, every ad-funded order loses money before a single fixed cost is counted. It's set by your margin — not by your industry, and not by the platform.

At 25% it's losing roughly half of every rupee. A ROAS quoted without the margin beside it carries no information.

Contribution margin, where the two differ materially. Gross margin usually subtracts only cost of goods — leaving out shipping and fulfilment, gateway fees, marketplace commissions, discounts, refunds and variable servicing costs. That produces a break-even ROAS that's too low, so a campaign can clear it and still lose money on every order.
