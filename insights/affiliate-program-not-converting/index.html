Why your affiliate program isn't driving revenue (and how to fix it)

Plenty of partners, plenty of clicks, plenty of dashboard activity — and almost no revenue. If that's your affiliate channel, the problem usually isn't the partners. It's how the program was built.

Anuja Founder, Tilth · 10+ years in performance marketing

Plenty of partners, plenty of clicks, plenty of dashboard activity — and almost no revenue. If that is your affiliate channel, the problem usually is not the partners. It is how the program was built: who was recruited, what the payout rewards, and whether anyone checks what the channel actually contributes.

1. You recruited for reach, not fit

The fastest way to fill a program is to accept everyone. It is also the fastest way to make it meaningless. Reach tells you how many people a partner can put in front of your offer; fit tells you whether those people were ever going to buy. A partner with 50,000 followers in an unrelated category will send traffic that bounces. A partner with 3,000 readers who already trust them on your category will send fewer visits and more customers.

Segment your partner list before you judge it. Content and review partners who explain the product, comparison sites that capture active research, loyalty and cashback partners who sit at the end of a journey, and coupon partners who intercept checkout are four different businesses. They deserve different payouts, different expectations and different limits — not one blanket commission.

2. Signups are not activation

Most programs report partner count. Almost none report how many partners have ever driven a sale. Those are different numbers and only one of them matters. Split your roster into partners who have never sent a conversion, partners who sent one and stopped, and partners producing repeatably. The third group is your actual program; the first group is a mailing list.

Activation is a job, not an outcome. A partner who joined and never received creative, a product feed, a working link or a single piece of guidance has not failed — they were never onboarded. Before concluding that affiliate does not work for you, check how many of your partners got as far as a usable link.

3. The payout rewards the wrong action

If you pay on click, you have bought traffic. If you pay on signup, you have bought form fills. Whatever you attach money to is what you will receive, in volume, regardless of whether it ever becomes revenue. Pay on the event that represents a customer — a completed order, a retained subscription, a qualified opportunity — and accept that the reported numbers will get smaller and more honest.

Design the payout event deliberately. Decide when it fires, what cancels it, and how long you wait before it is payable. A hold period long enough to cover your refund and cancellation window converts a lot of theoretical commission into commission you would actually have wanted to pay.

4. No one back-calculates payout against margin

A 20% commission is only generous or ruinous relative to your contribution margin after cost of goods, payment fees, shipping, returns and discounting. Work out what one order actually leaves you before you decide what a partner should earn from it — and do it per segment, because the partner who introduced a new customer and the partner who appeared beside your checkout are not delivering the same thing.

This is the same arithmetic that decides whether paid media is profitable, and it fails the same way. If you have not done it for your ad channels either, start with auditing whether your ad spend is actually profitable and reuse the margin numbers here.

A program that pays for the last click before checkout is not acquiring customers. It is renting a discount to people who had already decided.

5. Last-click attribution is paying for interception

Under pure last-click, whoever touches the journey closest to the order gets paid. That systematically rewards coupon, cashback and loyalty partners for customers your other channels already won, and systematically starves the content partners who created the demand. The channel then looks efficient in the affiliate dashboard while adding very little the business would have missed.

You do not need a perfect model to fix this. You need to know which model you are paying under, see where partners sit in real journeys, and be willing to pay differently for introduction than for interception. If attribution is the part you are least sure of, how attribution models actually differ is the shorter read.

6. Invalid and duplicate conversions go unchallenged

Affiliate is the channel where tracking problems quietly become invoices. Watch for the same order credited twice through different links, conversions recorded against partners whose click arrived after the order, traffic patterns that convert far outside your normal behaviour, and self-referred purchases. None of these require bad intent to appear — a misconfigured tag or a duplicated postback is enough.

Put a validation step between recorded conversion and payable commission: deduplicate on your own order ID, reconcile partner-reported conversions against your backend, and treat anything you cannot match as unpaid until it is explained. If your conversion data is not trustworthy on your own site yet, that is the prior problem — our growth strategy and measurement work exists because almost every channel decision downstream depends on it.

7. There is no vetting in, and no pruning out

Programs decay when joining is easy and leaving never happens. Set entry rules — category relevance, how the partner describes you, what traffic sources they use, whether they bid on your brand terms — and then review against them on a schedule. Monthly is enough. Look at contribution, not activity: revenue after the hold period, new versus returning customers, refund rate, and whether the partner would have been paid under a model you believe in.

When affiliate should not be scaled yet

Affiliate amplifies whatever system it is attached to, including a broken one. Do not scale it while any of the following is true: you cannot attribute a sale to a partner reliably, you do not know your contribution margin per order, your payout fires on something other than revenue, you have no hold period against refunds, or nobody owns weekly partner review. Fix those first and a modest program will outperform a large one.

How to rebuild it

Start with the roster: segment partners, identify the ones who have ever produced revenue, and onboard or retire the rest. Then fix the payout — correct event, margin-aware rate per segment, hold period that matches your refund window. Then fix measurement so you can see introduction separately from interception and deduplicate against your own orders. Only then recruit, and recruit for fit.

That sequence is the whole job, and it is the one we run in affiliate and partnership marketing: rebuild the economics and the tracking first, then grow the partner base against numbers you can defend. If you would rather pressure-test your own program before involving anyone, the sections above are the order to do it in.
