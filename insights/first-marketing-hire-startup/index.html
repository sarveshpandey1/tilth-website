Your startup's first marketing hire: who to hire first

Founders usually ask "SEO or paid ads person?" That's the wrong first question. Here's how to work out what's actually missing, what the Indian pay market really looks like once you read the sources properly, and how to set the hire up so it doesn't quietly fail.

Anuja Founder, Tilth · 10+ years in performance marketing

Most founders make the same mistake with their first marketing hire: they hire for the channel they're most worried about. Losing on SEO, so they hire an SEO person. Paid ads feel expensive, so they hire a media buyer. Six months later, half the marketing function has no owner and the specialist they hired is stuck waiting on tracking, content, and strategy that nobody else is doing. Your startup's first marketing hire needs to be built for a stage where almost nothing exists yet — not for a single channel.

The short answer: hire a generalist, not a specialist

Your first marketing hire should be a generalist growth marketer — someone who can run a small paid ads test, write landing page copy, fix a broken GA4 event, and report honestly on what worked, often in the same week. Not because specialists aren't valuable, but because at the stage of "we have one marketing hire," there isn't yet enough volume in any single channel to justify a narrow specialist. A brilliant SEO hire is wasted if nobody is fixing tracking, writing ad copy, or running the two or three channels you actually need to test first.

This generalist is sometimes called a "founding marketer" or "growth marketer" — typically someone who has touched growth, content and paid channels at a previous startup and is comfortable being hands-on rather than directing an agency. A generalist by necessity, not because they failed to specialise.

Diagnose what's missing before you write a job description

The useful version of this decision starts with what's actually broken, not with a title. Six questions settle most of it:

  • What is the founder bottleneck? If marketing stalls because nobody has time, you need hands. If it stalls because nobody knows what to do next, hands won't help.
  • Is strategy missing, or execution? These are different hires at different prices, and conflating them is the most common first-hire mistake.
  • Does acquisition already work at all? A hire who inherits one channel that converts has a very different job from one asked to find the first.
  • How many channels does the business genuinely need? Two done properly beats five done badly, and it changes whether one person is enough.
  • How mature is your tracking? If GA4, the ad pixels and your CRM disagree about what a conversion is, the first quarter goes on fixing measurement rather than growth. Worth knowing before you hire, not after.
  • Who produces the creative? Paid social in particular runs on a creative cadence. If nobody can make new assets, the hire inherits a production problem dressed as a channel problem.

Answer those honestly and you'll often find one person can't realistically cover the required disciplines — which is a useful finding, not a failure. The agency vs freelancer vs in-house question and the "who's my first hire" question are the same decision from two angles.

When an agency or freelancer is the better interim move

Hiring is slow and hard to reverse. If the gap is one bounded skill, if you need several disciplines but only a few days a month of each, or if you don't yet know which channel deserves a salary, external help buys the answer before you commit to payroll. Especially when the immediate job is diagnostic rather than operational — working out why acquisition isn't converting is measurement work, and it doesn't need a permanent hire.

When to make the hire

Hire too early and you're paying someone to market a product that hasn't found its shape. Hire too late and growth stalls with a founder stretched across product, sales and marketing. As a rough guide:

  • Too early: before you have signal on who buys and why. Marketing can't fix an unclear product.
  • About right: early traction, a repeatable if manual sales or signup motion, and a founder who can no longer keep up with content, ads and reporting alongside everything else.
  • Too late: a year of ad hoc ads and content with nobody accountable for whether any of it worked.

What to pay — and why the published numbers disagree

We used to print a salary table here. Checking the current sources made it look more confident than the evidence behind it: there's no authoritative salary survey for this role in India, the public figures disagree materially, and most describe a growth marketing manager at a funded company rather than a startup's first generalist. What you're really choosing between is source types:

Source typeWhat it actually tells you
Salary-platform model estimatesModelled from broad datasets. Currently put average base pay above ₹20 lakh — but for a manager at an established employer, not your first hire.
Crowd-sourced profile averagesSelf-reported, often from tiny samples. Directionally interesting, statistically thin.
Job-posting aggregatesClosest to what's actually being offered now, and usually lower than platform models. The most useful of the four for a startup.
Content marketing "salary guides"Published by firms with an interest in the number. Commentary, not data.

Source types, not a rate card. When public sources disagree this much, the honest move is to read live postings rather than average weak data into a false average.

Two things survive the uncertainty. Benchmark against live postings for your own city, stage and funding position — a seed-stage startup in Bengaluru and a Series C firm in Gurgaon aren't bidding in the same market for the same title. And budget total employer cost, not headline CTC: tools, devices, recruitment fees and the management time the role consumes are real money.

One pattern holds across every source: a deep specialist costs more than the generalist you need first — a second reason to sequence specialists later. If the maths feels tight against your overall marketing budget, work it out before you post the job.

The best first marketing hire isn't the person who knows one channel best. It's the person who can find out which channel is worth knowing best.

Red flags when hiring your first marketer

  • They can't explain how they'd measure success before you've told them your goals.
  • Their portfolio is all execution, no strategy — fine for a fourth hire, risky for a first.
  • They've only ever worked inside a large marketing team with dedicated designers, analysts and ops — a very different job from doing all of it themselves.
  • They're vague about tracking and attribution — they'll scale spend on numbers nobody checked.

What the first 90 days should actually produce

Agree this before the offer, because a first marketer with no defined output drifts into activity. A reasonable ninety days ends with measurement you both trust, a written view of who buys and why drawn from real customers, two or three channels tested small enough to afford and long enough to read, and an honest recommendation about where the next rupee goes. Note what isn't on that list: a content calendar, a rebrand, a headline growth number. Those are outputs of a working system, not evidence you're building one.

How to set them up to succeed

The single biggest reason first marketing hires fail isn't the person — it's that they inherit a foundation nobody has checked. Tracking that double-counts conversions, ad accounts with stale pixels, unclear attribution between paid and organic. A strong generalist will eventually notice and fix these things, but every month spent debugging measurement is a month not spent testing channels. An independent read on tracking and targeting before they start is the cheapest way to make sure their first quarter is spent growing the business rather than untangling it — and if the gap turns out to be broader than one person, our growth marketing services page is a fair checklist of what the function has to cover eventually, whoever ends up owning it.

First marketing hire — FAQs

Almost always a generalist growth marketer, not a channel specialist. Your first hire needs to run paid ads, write copy, fix tracking, and report on what's working, often in the same week. A narrow specialist usually leaves gaps nobody else on the team can fill.

A generalist, for the first hire specifically. Specialists earn more and go deeper on one channel, but a small startup doesn't yet have enough volume in any single channel to justify one. Bring in specialists once the generalist has proven which channels work.
