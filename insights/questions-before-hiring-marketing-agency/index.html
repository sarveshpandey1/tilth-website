12 questions to ask before hiring a marketing agency

The pitch always sounds good. The questions below are how you find out what's underneath it — on strategy, reporting, team, and terms. For each one, here's the answer you want to hear, and the red flag that should make you pause.

Anuja Founder, Tilth · 10+ years in performance marketing

Choosing a marketing agency is mostly an exercise in seeing past a good deck. Almost every agency presents well; far fewer do the unglamorous foundation work that decides whether your spend is measurable. These are the questions I'd ask before hiring a marketing agency — grouped by what they reveal — with the green-flag answer and the red flag for each.

Questions about process

01.What exactly will exist at day 30, 60 and 90?

Green flag: named deliverables with dates — measurement verified, a tested hypothesis list, a first read on which channel clears your economics.

Red flag: "we'll launch campaigns in week one", or a timeline made of activity rather than outputs you could check.

02.How will you verify my tracking before you scale anything?

Green flag: they treat tracking and attribution as step one and describe how — a test transaction, event-by-event checks, reconciliation against your backend.

Red flag: a blank look, or "tracking's already fine" without looking. Scaling on broken measurement just wastes money faster.

03.How do you decide what to test, and what would make you stop a test?

Green flag: a hypothesis, a decision rule agreed in advance, and a willingness to call something dead. Ask what they killed last quarter.

Red flag: "we're always testing" with no example of a test that failed and what it changed.

Questions about measurement & ownership

04.Whose name is on the ad accounts, analytics, pixels and creative files?

Green flag: yours, with the agency given access. Confirmed in writing, including what happens on exit.

Red flag: accounts in the agency's name, or raw platform data available only through their dashboard. That's leverage, not convenience.

05.Which system is the source of truth — and what do you do when it disagrees with the platform?

Green flag: one named system, usually the one closest to money, and a calm explanation that platforms over-claim and some gap is normal.

Red flag: whichever number looks best that month, or surprise that the two don't match.

06.What metrics will you report, and how often?

Green flag: outcomes tied to revenue or qualified pipeline, on a regular cadence, with the "so what" explained.

Red flag: impressions, reach and follower counts — metrics that move without the business moving.

Questions about the team

07.Who actually works on my account day to day?

Green flag: you're introduced to the real account manager and can gauge their seniority.

Red flag: the work quietly drops to junior staff the moment you sign.

08.Is the person in this pitch the one managing the work?

Green flag: yes, or a clear, named handover to someone you've met.

Red flag: the impressive strategist vanishes after the contract is signed.

09.What happens when something underperforms?

Green flag: a calm description of how they diagnose, reallocate, and communicate bad weeks.

Red flag: the implication that nothing ever underperforms.

Questions about terms & fit

10.What's the contract length, and what comes with me if I leave?

Green flag: a clear exit, no hidden off-boarding fees, and accounts, data, documentation and creative files that transfer.

Red flag: long lock-ins, punitive cancellation clauses, or a shrug about what you keep. Notice periods vary; what you walk away with matters more than the number of days.

11.What's excluded from the fee?

Green flag: a straight list — media spend, creative production, landing pages, tools, tracking work — and who pays for each. (More in what a marketing agency costs in India.)

Red flag: spend bundled into the fee so you can't see media versus management. If the fee is a percentage of spend, ask directly how they handle advising you to spend less — the honest ones have already thought about it.

12.Can you show results from businesses like mine, and how were they measured?

Green flag: relevant examples tied to revenue, and a straight explanation of the measurement behind them.

Red flag: big numbers with no context, no similar businesses, and no answer on how anything was tracked. An unexplained result is a claim, not evidence.

The best answers all point the same way: foundation first, honesty about measurement, and the same senior people after the ink dries.

You don't need all twelve, and a polished agency will answer several well. The ones that separate firms are 04, 05 and 11 — ownership, source of truth, and what the fee excludes — because they're hard to answer from a script and checkable later. If you're still weighing the model rather than the firm, we've compared agency, freelancer and in-house; and if nobody yet knows what's broken, that diagnosis is measurement work before it's a hiring decision.

Hiring a marketing agency — FAQs

The ones that are hard to answer from a script: who owns the ad accounts and analytics during and after the contract, which system is the source of truth when it disagrees with the platform, what is excluded from the fee, what the first 30/60/90 days produce, how they decide what to test and when to stop, who actually does the work, and how any result they show you was measured.

Jumping straight to tactics with no discovery, reporting only vanity metrics, refusing to fix tracking before scaling, a salesperson who disappears after signing, guaranteed results, and bundling ad spend into the fee so you can't see what's media versus management.

Yes. If tracking and attribution are wrong, every decision is based on bad data and scaling spend wastes money faster. A good agency fixes the measurement foundation first.

There's no standard, and notice periods vary. What matters more than the number is that the exit is clear, there are no hidden off-boarding fees, and the accounts, data and creative files come with you. Be cautious of long lock-ins with punitive cancellation clauses before anything's been proven.

Ask for results from similar businesses, tied to revenue rather than impressions, and ask how they were tracked. If they can't explain the measurement, treat the result as marketing, not proof.
