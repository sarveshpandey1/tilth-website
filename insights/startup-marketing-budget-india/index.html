Marketing budget for an early-stage startup in India

"What percentage should we spend on marketing?" is the question every founder asks first — and it's the wrong one to start with. Here's how to actually set a marketing budget for an early-stage startup in India: what the budget is really made of, the numbers that should size it, and how to budget without setting fire to your runway.

Anuja Founder, Tilth · 10+ years in performance marketing

Every early-stage founder wants a clean answer: "spend X% of revenue and you're done." The benchmarks exist, and they're a useful gut-check — but if you set your marketing budget off a percentage alone, you'll either starve a channel that was about to work or pour money into one you can't even measure. This is how to set a startup marketing budget that's tied to reality, not a rule of thumb.

First: a marketing budget isn't one number

Most budget arguments are really definition arguments, because "we spend ₹2 lakh a month on marketing" can mean five different things:

  • Media spend — paid to Google, Meta and others to buy reach. The only line that scales roughly with growth.
  • People and agency cost — salaries, retainers, freelancers. Mostly fixed, and slowest to change.
  • Creative and production — the assets the media spend needs. Routinely forgotten, then raided when something has to give.
  • Tools — analytics, automation, SEO, design. Small individually, surprising in aggregate.
  • Experimentation reserve — money allocated to finding out rather than performing. If it isn't named, it doesn't exist.

Media spend isn't the marketing budget; it's one line inside the marketing operating cost. Teams that conflate the two usually discover their real spend is well above what they thought, in the month they try to cut it.

Why "% of revenue" is the wrong place to start

Percentage-of-revenue rules get quoted constantly and the quoted figures vary widely, because they're a planning convention borrowed from established companies rather than a finding about startups. The deeper problem is structural: a budget is an input you control, revenue is an output you're trying to grow. Anchoring one to the other means under-investing exactly when you most need to find a channel, and over-investing the moment a good month tempts you to scale something unproven. A percentage is a late-stage sanity check on a number you reached another way — not the method.

Don't budget a percentage of revenue you don't have yet. Budget the number of customers you want — and what each one actually costs.

The three numbers that should size it

Contribution margin comes first: what's left from a sale after the costs of delivering it. That's the money available to buy a customer, and it's why two businesses with identical revenue can afford very different budgets.

Target CAC follows from it — the most you can pay to win a customer and still be building something. Multiply your realistic monthly customer target by that figure, add people, creative and tools, and you have a budget grounded in your own economics.

Payback period is the one founders skip and later regret: how many months of margin it takes to earn that acquisition cost back. The 3:1 LTV:CAC ratio everyone quotes is a useful convention rather than an established threshold, and it says nothing about timing. A healthy ratio with a payback period longer than your runway is still a cash problem. The catch: a budget is only as good as your tracking

Here's where most early-stage marketing budgets quietly fail. You can size everything perfectly around margin and CAC — but if your conversion tracking is wrong, your CAC is fiction and every allocation decision after it is a guess. Measurement readiness isn't a nice-to-have at this stage; it's the difference between a budget and a gamble, and getting it right is measurement work before it's a spending decision. It's exactly what a foundation audit checks first.

Test budget and scale budget are different money

A test budget buys information: enough spend, concentrated enough, to find out whether a channel can work. A scale budget buys customers from a channel you've already proven. They have different success criteria and shouldn't be reported together — a test that produces a clear "no" has done its job, and judging it on CAC will teach you to stop testing.

Practically: go deep on one or two channels rather than thin across six, ring-fence a smaller amount for genuine experiments, and keep a modest consistent line for things that compound slowly. Proportions depend on your stage, whether demand for your category already exists, and your sales cycle — a sixty-day cycle means a thirty-day test measures leads, not customers. Deciding that deliberately rather than by default is the core of performance marketing planning. Mistakes that waste an early budget

  • Spending before tracking works — you scale on numbers you can't trust.
  • Copying a percentage from a blog without checking your own margin, CAC and runway.
  • No ring-fenced test budget — so you never learn, or one bad test scares you off the channel.
  • Scaling on an unproven CAC after one good week.
  • Spreading thin across every channel instead of proving one.

A simple way to set your first budget

Work out your contribution margin. Set a target cost per customer inside it. Pick a realistic monthly customer target and multiply. Add people, creative and tools. Name an experimentation reserve. Check the payback period against your runway, cap the total at what you can afford to lose while learning, and fix tracking before you spend any of it. That's a budget grounded in your business — not someone else's percentage.
